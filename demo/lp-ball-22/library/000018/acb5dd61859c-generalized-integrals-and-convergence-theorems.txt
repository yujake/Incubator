Abstract. Under a wide concept of generalized integrals, classical results
like the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem and the rearrangement
Riemann theorem are presented and proven. At the end of the paper, an
application of the rearrangement theorem provides us with more informa-
tion about the connection between generalized and improper integrals.
Mathematics Subject Classiﬁcation. 40A10, 28C15.
Keywords. Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, Riemann rearrange-
ment theorem, improper integral, non-atomic measure.
1. Introduction
Given a positive measure space (X, M, μ), traditional deﬁnitions of improper
integrals have been taken historically as limits of integral values on sequences
of measurable sets that increase following prescribed rules up to cover μ−a.e.
the whole domain of integration. This approach includes the classical deﬁnition
of convergent series, where the partial sums are considered as integral values
with respect to the counting measure, and also includes integrals of functions
such as (1/x) sin x over the whole real line. However, these traditional deﬁ-
nitions of improper integrals are not the most suitable for recovering a given
function from its derivative or its Fourier series, for instance. Consequently,
other methods for deﬁning generalized integrals have been developed.
Denjoy [3,4] solved the problem of recovering functions from their deriva-
tives by developing a complicated integration method known as totalization.
Later, other approaches were proposed to solve this and other problems with

the use of diﬀerent methods of integration, for example the ones of Perron and

integrable functions, this paper adopts the broader term generalized integrals,
which includes the improper ones. However, it will become clear later that
many generalized integrals are also improper ones. This motivates the search
for some reasonable and uniﬁed meaning of generalized integrals that could be
applicable to most common cases, even when diﬀerentiation is not the main
point or perhaps has no sense.
In this regard, we aim to revitalize such a deﬁnition introduced in Jim´enez-
Pozo [9], which, as we shall see, it is satisﬁed by many diﬀerent constructions
of integrals. The main point is that all generalized integrals included in this
deﬁnition satisfy diﬀerent desired properties of the common integrals. The goal
of the present paper is just to prove these facts in the context of some classical
convergence theorems.

2. Preliminaries
Let us precise brieﬂy how we shall understand some basic concepts and symbols
used in measure and integration theory.
In dealing with topological measures if a complete information is required,
we shall denote by (X, d, O, M, μ) a nontrivial metric locally compact, σ-ﬁnite,
regular Borel measure space, where d is the distance that deﬁnes the open sets
O = O (X) . The elements of the σ−algebra generated by O are the Borel sets,
and M is the μ−completion of these Borel sets. Thus, μ is complete on M.
The symbol F (respectively K) stands for the class of all closed (respectively
compact) subsets of X. That the measure is Borel means μ (K) < ∞for
every K ∈K, and regularity properties are the usual ones associated to Radon
measures. The linear space of all real-valued μ−measurable functions on X,
will be denoted by M. But, as usual, M is also assumed to be the equivalence
classes of equal functions μ −a.e. The characteristic function of a set A is the
function 1A with constant values 1 on A and 0 on Ac. Other notations will
have their traditional meaning.
To plunge into our goals, a ﬁrst question is what could be (if any) an
acceptable deﬁnition for the meaning of generalized integral. It must be large
enough to cover most of the existent methods of summation of series and
improper integration, while not so much that it loses desirable properties. It
will also be convenient to have a presentation that allows for the direct use of
the long list of known theorems in measure and integration theory. We shall
risk employing the following one.

Denote by C = CT , the class of positive measure spaces on which a gener-
alized integral T will be deﬁned. For instance, numerable sets with the counting
measure, the class of real intervals with the Lebesgue measure, or the class of
compact sets with topological measures.
Deﬁnition 1 (Jim´enez-Pozo [9]). We shall say that (T, E) is a “generalized
integral” related to a positive measure space (X, M, μ) in a given class C, if
E ⊂M is a linear space, T is a linear functional on E, and the following
conditions are satisﬁed:
i) L1 (μ) ⊂E.
ii) If f ∈E and f ≥0, then f ∈L1(μ).
iii) If f ∈L1 (μ), then T(f) =

fdμ.
The notation (T, E) can be simpliﬁed by T and the functions (or classes
of functions) in E can be identiﬁed by calling them T-integrables and denoting
the functional T by the usual symbols of integrals. In the same way, in dealing
with counting measures and series, instead of “generalized integrals” we may
say “generalized summation methods” and so on.
Examples of generalized integrals in the class C of closed real intervals
include the Cauchy principal value; Denjoy, Perron, Henstock and Kurzweil
integrals, as well as the C-integral [1,2]. Other examples are the usual sum of
a series or its Ces`aro summability.

4. Rearrangement Riemann Theorems
The elementary yet surprising rearrangement Riemann theorem states that
any convergent series of real numbers that is not absolutely convergent (i.e.,
conditional convergent) can be rearranged to converge to any prescribed num-
ber or to oscillate, diverge, and so on. It is easy to reformulate and then extend
the theorem to series with coeﬃcients in the complex domain or even in the
euclidean m−dimensional space. The study of the subject in the case of series
with coeﬃcients in other spaces is very interesting, but it is out of the scope
of this paper.
Coming back to the classical theorem, the ingredients that allow its proof
are two: ﬁrst, both series formed by the positive and negative coeﬃcients re-
spectively of the original series are divergent. Second, the sequence of coeﬃ-
cients of the series converges to 0. Then, the Riemann theorem applies to any
real series that satisﬁes both conditions, even if it is not convergent in the
usual sense. Thus, due to this remark and for further use, denote by l∞

Ending Comments
In the framework of the Henstock-Kurzweil integral over real intervals, it is
claimed from the renowned Hake’s theorem [6] that, the class of Henstock-
Kurzweil integrable functions cannot be enlarged by using the same idea be-
hind classical improper integration (see also [5], Theorem 9.21 pp. 150). How-
ever, we have seen that the existence of any generalized integral of a non-
absolutely integrable function implies neither the positive nor the negative
part of the function is integrable, and then Theorem 12 is applicable. The-
orem 12 also guarantees that, under additional hypothesis, any generalized
integral is, in some sense, an improper one.
Theorem 12 is an existence result and does not indicate how to choose
the claimed open sets (Remark 13). Nevertheless, we can ﬁx classes of open
sets and use improper integrals to deﬁne generalized ones. We shall come back
to exploit this approach in another paper in order to revitalize generalized
integrals in the large class of topological measures.
