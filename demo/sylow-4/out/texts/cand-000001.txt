Statement of the theorems
Let G be a ﬁnite group of order n. Let p be a prime that divides n, and
suppose pr|n but pr+1 does not divide n; we often write pr||n.
Theorem (Sylow theorems)
(1) G contains a subgroup H ⊂G of order pr. Any such group is
called a Sylow p-subgroup.
(2) All Sylow p-subgroups of G are conjugate by elements of G. In
particular, if G has only one Sylow p-subgroup H, then H is a
normal subgroup of G.
(3) The number of distinct Sylow p-subgroups of G is (i) congruent
to 1 modulo p and (ii) divides |G|.

Some examples
Some applications
Example
Let G be a group of order 21 = 3 · 7 and let a be the number of its
distinct Sylow 7-subgroups. By the Third Sylow Theorem a ≡1
(mod 7) and a divides 21. The only divisor of 21 congruent to 1
modulo 7 is 1. So a = 1 and its unique Sylow 7-subgroup is normal.
More generally,
Proposition
Let G be a group of order pq, where p and q are distinct primes,
q > p. Then G has a unique Sylow q-subgroup H and H is normal.
Moreover H is cyclic and if p does not divide q −1 then G is abelian.

Proof.
If a is the number of Sylow q-subgroups of G, then a divides pq and
a ≡1 (mod q). The only divisors of pq are 1, p, q, and pq, and of
those only 1 ≡1 (mod q), because p < q.
By the Second Sylow Theorem, the unique Sylow q-subgroup H is
normal. Since H is of order q it is abelian. Now consider the
conjugation action of G on the normal subgroup H: it deﬁnes a
homomorphism c : G →Aut(H) = Z×

gcd(|G|, q −1) = gcd(pq, q −1) = gcd(p, q −1), since q is

Some examples
The case of S4
If G = S4 then |G| = 24 = 23 × 3. We know that any 3 cycle
generates a Sylow 3-subgroup. What is the structure of a Sylow
2-subgroup?
A Sylow 2-subgroup of A4 is isomorphic to the Klein group K4, and
contains all the elements with cycle decomposition 2 + 2. This set is
invariant under conjugation by S4, so K4 is a normal subgroup of S4.
Let s = (12) ∈S4 (or any 2 cycle), H the subgroup generated by s.
Then P = H · K4 is a Sylow 2-subgroup of order 8 with a normal
subgroup of index 2.

Theorem
Let G be a ﬁnite group of order n and let p be a prime dividing n.
Then G has an element of order p.
Proof.
We use the Class Equation, where the xi are representatives of
conjugacy classes not in the center:
|G| = |Z(G)| +

[G : Cxi]
Assume the theorem is true for groups of order less than n. If p divides
the order of one of the Cxi, then by induction Cxi has an element g of
order p, because |Cxi| < |G| = n. But g ∈Cxi ⊆G, so we are done.

Proof of the First Sylow Theorem, continued
First suppose p does not divide [G : Cxi] for at least one i, hence pr
divides Cxi, which is of smaller order than G. By induction Cxi has a
subgroup of order pr.
So we may suppose p divides every [G : Cxi], hence p divides |Z(G)|.
Then Z(G) contains a subgroup N of order p (by classiﬁcation, or by
Cauchy’s theorem). But any subgroup of Z(G)| is normal in G
(exercise). So G/N is of order less than n and by induction contains a
subgroup ¯K of order pr−1. Let K ⊂G be the corresponding subgroup
containing N (the preimage of ¯K under the quotient map). Then
|K| = |N|¯K = p · pr−1 = pr.
and we are done.

Another lemma
Lemma
Suppose P ⊆G is a p-Sylow subgroup. Let g ∈NG(P) and assume g
has order pr for some r ≥1. Then g ∈P.
Proof.
Consider the subgroup ⟨gP⟩⊂NG(P)/P. If g /∈P then ⟨gP⟩has order
p. Let J ⊂NG(P) be the subgroup generated by g and P; so that
J/P = ⟨gP⟩.
On the other hand, the coset gP is the image of g under the map
J 7→J/P, and is thus of order pa for some a ≤r. If g /∈P it follows
that |J| = p · |P| > |P|. But |P| is the largest power of p dividing |G|.
Thus we must have J = P.
